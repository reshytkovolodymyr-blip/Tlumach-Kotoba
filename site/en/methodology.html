The Tlumach Kotoba method

Learn a language the living way

How the approach works

Tlumach Kotoba doesn't invent a new way to learn languages. It takes a few well-known, well-researched techniques and combines them into one clear, step-by-step process.

The idea is simple: instead of learning a language through separate words and rules, you work with one real dialogue many times. First you listen to it, then you repeat it together with the speaker, and finally you try to reproduce it on your own — without prompts.

This path draws on research into listening, repetition, shadowing, speaking practice and the gradual reduction of support while learning.

Learning through a whole dialogue

Every lesson is built around one specific dialogue, not a set of unrelated sentences.

This matters: in a real language, words and phrases don't appear on their own — they appear in situations. You hear who is speaking, how the conversation unfolds and how one line leads to the next. This helps you see the language as a living system people actually use, not a list of isolated rules to memorize.

Language-teaching research shows that working with the same material repeatedly, and in context, supports understanding and long-term memory better than drilling isolated pieces taken out of their situation.

The sequence of exercises

Step 1 — Full Dialogue

First you simply listen to the whole dialogue at natural speed.

At this stage you don't need to repeat or recall anything. The only task is to get used to how the language sounds: its rhythm, intonation and speed. This gives you a first overall sense of the dialogue before going into detail.

Step 2 — Shadowing, pass 1 (slow)

After that first listen, shadowing begins — at a slower speed than natural.

You hear a line and repeat it right after the speaker or together with them. The slower pace lets you focus on pronunciation, rhythm and the structure of the phrase at the start, without being overwhelmed by the speed of real speech.

Research on shadowing, including work by Hamada and others, shows that the technique is used to train both listening and speaking at the same time.

Step 3 — Shadowing, pass 2 (natural speed)

The second pass runs at natural speaking speed.

By now the dialogue is familiar, so you can move from slow, controlled repetition to normal speed. Your attention shifts to how the language sounds in real conversation — not in a simplified classroom version.

Step 4 — Shadowing, pass 3 (no pause)

The third pass is the hardest.

You repeat the speech almost at the same time as the speaker, with no separate pause to prepare. This is no longer just repeating a phrase you heard — it trains you to hear and reproduce speech almost instantly, which is one of the key features of classic shadowing.

Why three passes

The three passes aren't there to make you mechanically listen to the same text three times. Each stage reduces the support you get:

  1. First you only listen — no pressure to reproduce anything.
  2. Then you get a slow, very clear model.
  3. Next the speed rises to natural.
  4. Finally there's hardly any time to think — you have to react almost automatically.

This follows the idea of scaffolding — support for the learner that is deliberately reduced over time. The approach goes back to classic learning research, and modern language teaching uses it widely to move from working with prompts to doing things fully on your own.

Why active recall comes after listening

Listening many times isn't enough for a language to stay in your memory for long.

So once you've taken the material in, the next logical step is active practice: not just recognizing a phrase among what you've heard, but producing it from memory yourself. That's what the Recall mode is for — a line from the dialogue plays, then there's a pause to remember, and only then do you hear the reply from the dialogue itself. Instead of simply recognizing familiar information (a much easier, more passive task), your brain has to actively pull out the logic of the conversation — to guess where the dialogue goes next before hearing the right answer.

Memory and learning research consistently shows that actively retrieving information is a far more effective path to lasting memory than passively going over the same material again.

Why one dialogue is used in several modes

All exercise modes work with the same lesson. You don't jump to a new set of random sentences each time — the same material comes back, but with a different goal each time:

  • Full Dialogue — understand the overall meaning.
  • Shadowing — train listening, pronunciation and speech rhythm.
  • Key Phrases — practice the important phrases from the dialogue separately: first their form and sound, then the translation and an example of use.
  • Recall — check whether you can recall the logic of the dialogue — where the conversation goes next — without full prompts, entirely in the target language, with no translation.

This way one dialogue goes through several levels of processing — from a first overall impression to fully independent reproduction.

The role of translation

In this approach, translation is a helper, not a replacement for the foreign language.

You start by working directly with the sound and the dialogue itself. Translation comes in only when you really need to check a meaning or recover what was said.

There is one principle the app never breaks: it must not invent lesson content on its own. If a translation, explanation or any other language information appears in an exercise, it must come from the material of that specific lesson, provided by the user — not be made up by the app.

What Tlumach Kotoba actually does

Tlumach Kotoba doesn't claim to be a new scientific method of learning languages. Its job is different: to take known, proven learning techniques and make them genuinely convenient for everyday self-study.

An AI helps create the learning material — a real dialogue on the topic and level you choose. Tlumach Kotoba turns that material into a structured lesson and automatically builds the exercise modes, voicing, pauses and ready-made audio files.

In the end you get one dialogue and work through it — gradually, step by step — from simple listening to fully independent reproduction.

The core principle

The whole approach fits into one short sequence:

heard → understood → repeated → sped up → said it on your own

This sequence brings together ideas from research on shadowing, listening, active recall and the gradual reduction of learning support. It's the principle the Tlumach Kotoba method is built on.
